\section{Introduction}\label{s:intro}

\subsection{Background and Motivation}

Two or more pieces of software, such as languages following different design paradigms in their compilation approaches and linguistic properties, working together via some means of communication forms the basis of what most people would understand as software interoperability. Interoperability is no new concept; it ties quite naturally into how the software systems we interact with evolve. Abstraction has been central to the innovations in programming languages to align more intuitively with natural language and make them more efficient for programmers. As many new programming languages distanced themselves over time from the hardware they had to execute on, we had to develop systems that translate these composited abstractions, which define most software, back into the basic codes that machines can apply to execute said abstractions. Two notable byproducts of this type of evolution are that 

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Performance characteristics in kind evolved with the abstractions we developed to design new programming languages.
    \item Not all abstractions are created equal, so naturally, languages evolve in various directions, built on various execution models.
\end{enumerate}

A tradeoff between performance and ease-of-use can lead to a natural, albeit conflicting, question of how to balance ease-of-use abstractions and the desired performance characteristics of the granular low-level control of hardware. Introducing domain-specific or new language abstractions can additionally lead to questions about how language abstractions differ and in what ways these differences are more applicable to some domains than others. All these considerations- performance, ease of use, language-specific features, or, more abstractly, the unique and evolving qualities of programming languages- should then naturally lead us to why we might want to bridge specific attributes to "get the best of both worlds." 

A byproduct of the increasing number of programming languages, especially ones that improve upon older languages considerably in aspects such as memory safety and performance, has led to an increased effort to develop practical solutions for language interoperability both to create bridges to well-established legacy codebases and to gradually rewrite codebases to align with modern safety standards and performance needs. Accounting for this increase in programming languages, ahead-of-time (AOT) compiler designers generally adopted modular architecture to provide an extensible language development framework supporting various languages. Extensibility in AOT compilers typically follows the pattern of a front end, which a language developer must implement, some language-agnostic intermediate representation (IR) middle-end, such as GIMPLE for the GNU Compiler Collection (GCC) or LLVM-IR for the clang compiler, during which compiler and language developers can write generic or custom optimization passes, and a back-end during which the optimized IR is transformed into machine code to target some instruction set architecture (ISA). Separation of concerns: having a front, middle, and back-end with AOT compilation while providing flexibility can still be limited because we are confined to the implementation framework of the compiler. Interpreters address the constraints of AOT compilers by providing a mostly platform-agnostic runtime execution model that trades bare-metal performance for the increased flexibility of language design by abstracting away the difficulties of low-level considerations. Just-in-time (JIT) compilers aimed to leverage the runtime information gained from interpreted execution approaches and combine this with more traditional bare-metal compilation to have both runtime language dynamism and performance through paradigms such as Profile Guided Optimization (PGO) enabling specialized recompilation of \textit{hot}, frequently executed, code segments. Combining various execution models: interpreted, hardware compiled with IR and custom byte-code, and implementing runtime optimizations, usually means JIT compilers have a rather complex and bespoke pipeline leading to difficulties in their adaptation to a broader set of languages outside of the main set they where designed around. Increasing the modularity of JIT compilers or more generally the virtual machines (VMs) they are part of has been an active area of research and has had considerable innovations both evolving VM ecosystem's such as the Java VM (JVM) from Oracle with Java, Kotlin, and Scala in addition to providing frameworks to implement new languages such as the GraalVM \cite{graalvm2025introduction} or Microsofts Common Language Runtime (CLR) \cite{microsoft2023clr} C\#, F\# and Visual Basic .NET (VB.NET) with community projects for other languages like C++, JS, Python and so on.  
 
Reconciling the distinct qualities of AOT-compiled languages such as C++ and a modern JIT-compiled language such as Julia in the context of interoperability is a primary motivator for this paper. Further considerations are not only the practical ramifications of bridging Julia and C++ but also the insights we can gain from attempting to understand a more unified picture of how we can bridge this, in some ways, diverged evolution of different features of programming languages, especially in a contemporary context. We will consider two angles in this research. To provide a foundation for understanding the research's more practical and applied elements, we will elaborate on the background that underpins the most common form of interoperability and how this takes shape in modern interoperability tooling to contextualize the later case study into the broader landscape of the tooling. A grounded, more applied perspective will also be explored by performing a practical case study through the application and extension of an interoperability tool called Jluna for C++ and Julia. As both Julia and C++ have an overlap in being used in scientific and high-performance-computing (HPC) applications, this case study will specifically be centered around using C++ to implement a peformance-intensive segment of a ray-tracer implementation in Julia mirroring how one might use existing well-established C++ libraries to accelerate performance-critical segments of a larger application written in a more dynamic modern language such as Julia. 

\subsection{Objectives}

Primarily the objective of this work is a to present tool-based design and case study in applied contemporary language interoperability research with a focus on system design, practical implemenation and language semantics. Integral to understanding most concepts one learns throughout thier academic and professional career is building a mental structure for what defines the shape and various aspects of a specific domain. Interoperability, being an expansive domain, can then be difficult both to build a larger structure of and understand how it ties into some of the broader aspects of programming languages. A central takeaway we aim to establish here is that despite this expansive nature of the language interoperability domain and the seemingly irreconciliable attribuets of divergent language and compiler design paradigms there is a structure which can be understood and leveraged to extend tooling to better fit modern programming language semantics. Decomposing our objective into research we aim to explore the following

\subsubsection{Research questions}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item What are the foundations of FFI-based tooling, and how can WebAssembly (WASM) innovate on C-ABI based FFI approaches ? 
    \item How do interoperability tools address architectural patterns, memory management, and type representation challenges, and how do these strategies vary based on language and runtime similarity?
    \item How can we create a more formal and integrated understanding of interoperability tools ?
    \item What are some of the ramifications of extending existing tooling to support polymorphism across AOT and JIT compiled languages ?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Scope and Limitations}

FFI-based contemporary interoperability tools and related background and theory will be the main focus of the research. VM language implementation frameworks, such as GraalVM, which have appeared more recently will be mentioned though not thoughrougly evaluated. Additionally we will constrain ourselves to mainly tooling which was developed in the past 10 years or so and is mainly aimed at practical industry applications as opposed to more nieche use-cases. We will also elaborate on some necessary background to understand the basic technology this interop tooling is built on though with a focus on its application in a modern context and evaluating it alongside some potential modern sucessors. Theoretical formalisms will also be introduced; though primarily as a means of contextualizing some of the practical implementation considerations through a type theoretical lens; less so as a means of rigorous formal verification. Situating the extension case-study within the context of similar tooling and some more traditionally theoretical concepts is the predominant focus of this research.

\subsection{Structure of the paper}

We will start by introducing language interoperability foundations, practical considerations and some future facing technologies like WASM which could serve as new basis for modern tooling to asses RQ (1). Evaluating RQ (2) will occur in two parts first looking into some of the more general strategies and design patterns we see for languages of different degrees of overlap in their design and implementation; second focusing specifically on memory management strategies in the context of AOT, JIT and hybrid AOT and JIT compiled systems. How interoperability tools actually understand types they want to bridge and the relevance of reflection as a contrast to older approaches will be assesed for RQ (4). Finally RQ (3) and RQ (4) will be explored through the extension case-study by demonstrating a potentially more integrated understanding of binding generators and to evaluate the ramifications of encoding and implementing polymorphism between an AOT compiled language, C++, and a JIT compiled language, Julia.
