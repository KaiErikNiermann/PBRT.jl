\section{Introduction}\label{s:intro}

\subsection{Background and Motivation}

Two or more pieces of software, such as languages following different design paradigms in their compilation approaches and linguistic properties, working together via some means of communication forms the basis of what most people would understand as software interoperability. Interoperability is no new concept; it naturally ties into how the software systems we interact with evolve. Abstraction has been central to innovations in programming languages, aligning them more intuitively with natural language and making them more efficient for programmers. As many new programming languages distanced themselves over time from the hardware on which they had to execute, we had to develop systems that translate these composite abstractions, which define most software, back into the basic codes that machines can apply to execute said abstractions. Two notable byproducts of this type of evolution are that 
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Performance characteristics in kind evolved with the abstractions we developed to design new programming languages.
    \item Not all abstractions are created equal, so naturally, languages evolve in various directions, built on various execution models.
\end{enumerate}

A tradeoff between performance and ease of use can lead to a natural, albeit conflicting, question of how to balance ease-of-use abstractions and the desired performance characteristics of granular, low-level hardware control. Introducing domain-specific or new language abstractions can additionally lead to questions about how language abstractions differ and in what ways these differences are more applicable to some domains than others. All these considerations -performance, ease of use, language-specific features, or, more abstractly, the unique and evolving qualities of programming languages -should then naturally lead us to why we might want to bridge specific attributes to "get the best of both worlds." 

A byproduct of the increasing number of programming languages, especially ones that improve upon older languages considerably in aspects such as memory safety and performance, has led to an increased effort to develop practical solutions for language interoperability both to create bridges to well-established legacy codebases and to gradually rewrite codebases to align with modern safety standards and performance needs. Accounting for this increase in programming languages, ahead-of-time (AOT) compiler designers generally adopted a modular architecture to provide an extensible language development framework that supports various languages. Extensibility in AOT compilers typically follows the pattern of a front end, which a language developer must implement, some language-agnostic intermediate representation (IR) middle-end, such as GIMPLE for the GNU Compiler Collection (GCC) or LLVM-IR for the clang compiler, during which compiler and language developers can write generic or custom optimization passes, and a back-end during which the optimized IR is transformed into machine code to target some instruction set architecture (ISA). Separation of concerns: having a front-end, middle-end, and back-end with AOT compilation provides flexibility, but it can still be limited because we are confined to the implementation framework of the compiler. Interpreters address the constraints of AOT compilers by providing a platform-agnostic runtime execution model that trades bare-metal performance for increased flexibility in language design, abstracting away the difficulties of low-level considerations. Just-in-time (JIT) compilers aim to leverage the runtime information gained from interpreted execution approaches and combine this with more traditional bare-metal compilation to achieve both runtime language dynamism and performance through paradigms such as Profile Guided Optimization (PGO), enabling the specialized recompilation of \textit{hot}, frequently executed, code segments. Combining various execution models: interpreted, hardware compiled with IR and custom byte-code, and implementing runtime optimizations, usually means JIT compilers have a rather complex and bespoke pipeline leading to difficulties in their adaptation to a broader set of languages outside of the main set they where designed around. Increasing the modularity of JIT compilers or, more generally, the \textit{virtual machines} (VMs) they are part of has been an active area of research and has had considerable innovations in both evolving VM ecosystems such as the Java VM (JVM) from Oracle with Java, Kotlin, and Scala in addition to providing frameworks to implement new languages such as the GraalVM \cite{graalvm2025introduction} or Microsofts Common Language Runtime (CLR) \cite{microsoft2023clr} C\#, F\# and Visual Basic .NET (VB.NET) with community projects for other languages like C++, JS, Python and so on.  
 
Reconciling the distinct qualities of AOT-compiled languages, such as C++, with those of a modern JIT-compiled language, like Julia, in the context of interoperability is a primary motivator for this paper. Further considerations include not only the practical ramifications of bridging Julia and C++ but also the insights we can gain from attempting to understand a more unified picture of how to bridge this, in some ways, divergent evolution of different programming language features, especially in a contemporary context. We will consider two angles in this research. To provide a foundation for understanding the research's more practical and applied elements, we will elaborate on the background that underpins the most common form of interoperability and how this takes shape in modern interoperability tooling, thereby contextualizing the later case study within the broader landscape of the tooling. We will also explore a grounded, more applied perspective by performing a practical case study through the application and extension of an interoperability tool called Jluna for C++ and Julia. As both Julia and C++ have an overlap in being used in scientific and high-performance-computing (HPC) applications, this case study will specifically be centered around using C++ to implement a performance-intensive segment of a ray-tracer implementation in Julia mirroring how one might use existing well-established C++ libraries to accelerate performance-critical segments of a larger application written in a more dynamic modern language such as Julia. 

\subsection{Objectives}

Primarily, the objective of this work is to present a tool-based design and case study in applied contemporary language interoperability research with a focus on system design, practical implementation, and language semantics. Integral to understanding most concepts one learns throughout one's academic and professional career is building a mental structure for what defines the shape and various aspects of a specific domain. Interoperability, being an expansive domain, can then be difficult both to build a larger structure of and understand how it ties into some of the broader aspects of programming languages. A central takeaway is that, despite the expansive nature of the language interoperability domain and the seemingly irreconcilable attributes of divergent language and compiler design paradigms, a structure can be understood and leveraged to extend tooling to fit modern programming language semantics better. Decomposing our objective into research, we explore the following.

\subsubsection{Research questions}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item What are the foundations of FFI-based tooling, and how can WebAssembly (WASM) innovate on C-ABI-based FFI approaches? 
    \item How do interoperability tools approach system design, memory management, and type representation challenges, and how do these approaches vary based on language and runtime similarity?
    \item How can we discuss interoperability tools in the context of type theory, and how does this understanding inform the design of binding generators?
    \item What are some of the ramifications of extending existing tooling to support polymorphism across AOT and JIT compiled languages?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Scope and Limitations}

FFI-based contemporary interoperability tools and related background and theory will be the primary focus of the research. VM language implementation frameworks, such as the recent GraalVM, will be mentioned, though not thoroughly evaluated. Additionally, we will limit ourselves to the main tooling, which has been developed over the past 10 years or so and is primarily aimed at practical industry applications, as opposed to more niche use cases. We will also elaborate on some necessary background to understand the basic technology this interop tooling is built on, though, with a focus on its application in a modern context and evaluating it alongside some potential modern successors. We will also introduce theoretical formalisms, primarily as a means of presenting some of the practical implementation considerations through a theoretical lens rather than as a means of rigorous formal verification or proposing any new theorems. Situating the extension case study within the context of similar tooling and some more traditionally theoretical concepts is the predominant focus of this research.

\subsection{Structure of the paper}

We will begin by introducing the foundations of language interoperability, practical considerations, and some future-facing technologies, such as WASM, which could serve as a new basis for modern tooling to assess RQ (1). Evaluating RQ (2) will occur in three parts: first, examining some of the more general strategies and design patterns of interoperability tooling, we observe applied for languages with varying degrees of overlap in their design and implementation; second, focusing specifically on memory management strategies in the context of AOT, JIT, and hybrid AOT and JIT compiled interoperability systems. For the last part, regarding RQ (2), we will assess how interoperability tools understand the types they aim to bridge and the relevance of reflection in that process. Finally, RQ (3) and RQ (4) will be explored by applying some existing theory to the field of language interoperability and, via the extension case study, to evaluate the ramifications of encoding and implementing polymorphism between an AOT-compiled language, C++, and a JIT-compiled language, Julia.
