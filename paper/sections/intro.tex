% background and context

Abstractly speaking interoperability in the context of software is the simple notion of two or more systems, such as languages following different design paradigms in their execution models and linguistic properties, working together via some means of communication. Interoperability is by no means a new concept, in many ways, it ties quite naturally into how systems we interact with evolve. If we take the most relevant example of such systems, programming languages, we can note that their evolution over time has been based on the development of abstractions to make their usage easier for programmers. Naturally, with the evolution of these abstractions we developed systems that must translate composited abstractions which define most software back into the basic codes that machines can apply to execute said abstractions. Two notable byproducts of this type of evolution are that
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The performance characteristics in kind evolved with the abstractions we developed to design new programming languages.
    \item Not all abstractions are created equal, so naturally languages evolve in various directions, built on various execution models.
\end{enumerate}
From the first point, it would then be a natural question, sooner or later, for most programmers how one can create a balance between ease of use abstractions and still the desired performance characteristics of the granular low-level control of hardware through languages that allow such control. For the second point a question one might ask is in what way do these abstractions differ, and in what ways are these differences more applicable to some domains than others. For both of the above questions, I hope that it becomes clear how we then derive the desire for interoperability in software systems. 

In a more contemporary context, we have had a considerable evolution from the early days of interoperability. The increasing variations of hardware chipsets have led to a corresponding variety of instruction set architectures (ISAs). Similarly, when zooming out we have had a considerable increase in the number of programming languages, largely supported by improvements in language execution models. As languages and ISAs evolved compilers naturally adopted a modular or specialized architecture to better fit the various designs people wanted to implement. We saw the evolution of ahead-of-time (AOT) compilers used predominantly for statically typed languages with a greater focus on performance as opposed to language flexibility Likewise here we saw interoperability applied in various forms, most notably with the creation of LLVM Intermediate Representation (IR) the use of a language agnostic IR meant optimization passed could be performed on arbitrary languages as long as developers implemented the corresponding IR generation. We also saw the creation of just-in-time (JIT) compilers, which themselves provided an abstraction to interpret languages and compile them to machine code at runtime. This generally comes at the cost of some performance by opening the door to a substantial field of research into different types of optimizations one could apply given information only accessible at runtime. A notable downside with effective JIT compilers has been their historically complex nature and inherent lack of modularity. JIT compilers generally exist alongside a language runtime, examples being the JVM for Java, V8 for JavaScript, and CLR for C\#, to execute said languages with an emphasis on dynamic behavior through lacking the modularity in their ability to work with a wider variety of languages as contrasted with AOT compilers they generally have no analog to IR Nonetheless there has been a decently sized body of research dedicated into exploring improvements in both the performance characteristics of JIT compilers to more closely match those of AOT compilers, but also at various attempts for create language agnostic representations not only to yield a more modular architecture to support the development of new languages on the respective language platform but also to allow for effect polyglot (multi-language) systems whose dynamics are all nicely understood and defined within the context of some language runtime.
 
The initial idea I wanted to test came from a curiosity about the interactions between languages operating under different execution models, particularly the dynamics of the Julia JIT compiler and the Clang AOT compiler used with C++ in a practical context. This interest gradually evolved into a broader inquiry about software interoperability, particularly exploring its practical aspects and why they are intriguing and warrant further investigation. My research followed a two-pronged approach: the first being to develop a comprehensive understanding of the modern state of software interoperability and the diverse ways polyglot systems are implemented. Here, my goal was to present a broad structural overview, motivated by a desire to understand how my practical work fits within the larger field and how its techniques are compared to industry standards. The second prong involved acquiring hands-on knowledge by applying interoperability concepts to a specific case. I aimed to explore whether I could replace a performance-intensive segment of my program with one written in a different language, highlighting both the benefits and drawbacks of modern techniques compared to legacy methods. The crux was to bridge the gap between theoretical frameworks and practical applications, examining how abstract concepts contrast with real-world examples. Ultimately, this research sought to link and contrast various aspects of interoperability theory with an engaging practical case, providing insights into how these concepts connect and reinforce each other.

% -> Research aim and objectives

The primary aim of this work is to present a large structural overview of the modern state of software interop and to link and contrast various concepts from this with an applied practical example. The goal of this is to create a somewhat comprehensive overview of the world of language interoperability while providing a concrete example that applies some modern approaches. Similarly to various other fields in computer science interoperability nowadays forms a rather expansive collection of research and real-world/industry applications. While this is of course broadly speaking positive I believe there are insights to be gained from creating a more unified albeit constrained picture of the larger field. Generally speaking, it is not too uncommon that you can see a dichotomy form in different aspects of computer science between what works and has worked for a while; often taking the form of legacy systems which are iteratively patched to be good enough; and newer research which can often be somewhat inaccessible, complex to adapt, and difficult to see the real utility of. The same can indeed be seen for the case of software interoperability, thus with this work, I hope to gain but also provide some insights into how some of these newer developments in the design of programming languages, interoperability layers, and polyglot systems can potentially assist in creating more effective software solutions.
    
To put it more concretely, there are several objectives I aim to achieve with this work:
\begin{enumerate}[topsep=0pt]
    \item Research and present an overview of the modern state of language interoperability, with a focus on the more recent developments in the field and compare and contrast these findings with an applied example.
    \item Examine how various aspects of language interoperability have evolved together, thus presenting a more unified and interconnected view of the field.
    \item Assess the benefits and drawbacks of employing modern techniques, particularly focusing on language paradigms within interoperability, the performance dynamics in critical systems, and the influence of modern type systems on the design and application of interoperability frameworks.
    \item Using insights gained from both the theoretical and practical aspects to yield potential future directions for research and development in the field of software interoperability.
\end{enumerate}

% -> Scope and limitations 

One could theoretically go into an arbitrary depth when doing this type of exploration but due to the inherently flexible nature that exists not only within computer science but any field that has developed for so long there is value in creating a more abstract overview which can hopefully provide a more stable foundation over time. The primary limitation is that naturally with something as expansive as interoperability while there is the aim to document a wide portion of the field, not all aspects will be discussed, the focus will be on the modern state of the field as this isn't meant to be a historical document, rather are a review of the modern state of interoperability in software. The main purpose again is to provide the reader with both a broad overview of interoperability in software but also link this to a modern concrete example to present some potentially interesting new areas to explore especially in the context of evolving type systems of programming languages.

% -> research methodology

To form an outline of how this overview of interop. Will be approached it would be reasonable to build on the fundamental aspects of the field which act as the foundation for various legacy approaches in the implementation of polyglot (multi-language) systems. From this, we can expand to how higher levels of abstractions are applied in terms of both the evolution of programming languages specifically focusing on their type systems and the corresponding execution models. The methodology here will be focused on aggregating and reviewing various instances of research and applications to demonstrate this overview. A focus will be placed on specifically how various aspects of interoperability build on top of one another and tie into each other, with a secondary focus on ensuring that sufficient topics are introduced to provide the background knowledge required to understand the motivating practical example and give the reader the same insights into where it fits in the broader context of interoperability as I wished to gain with this research. Of note is that this is not meant to be a meta-analysis but rather a survey that is compared and contrasted against a concrete practical implementation.

% -> Structure of the thesis

The background and related works will first build upon some of the more low-level aspects of interop, mainly foreign function interfaces (FFI) and the corresponding machine code specifications known as application binary interfaces (ABIs). We will then shift to the more modern approaches employing various meta techniques largely revolving around the application of various types of abstractions to define different modes of interop. Finally, a motivating practical example will be presented which applies various of the topics introduced in the background to demonstrate their application in addition to contrasting this with various other approaches. The hope here is to provide not only a broad overview but also compare and contrast this with a practical example so the reader can gain a broad overview to explore some potentially interesting areas of interoperability, but also understands how various aspects of modern interoperability can have demonstrable real-world utility through an applied example and aren't only theoretical demonstrations.
